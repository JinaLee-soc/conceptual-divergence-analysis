# Novelty as Audience Repair: Instrument Time and the Ratification of Discovery at the Halvorsen Array

## Working draft — introduction and theoretical framing

The standard story about scientific novelty treats it as a property of ideas:
a finding either recombines what was previously separate or it does not, and
the recombination either lands or it does not. Eighteen months of fieldwork
among the operations staff of the Halvorsen Array, a mid-sized radio
observatory, convinced me that this story is backwards. At the Array, novelty
is not a property that a signal carries into the control room; novelty is a
status that audiences confer on a signal after they have finished repairing
their own expectations around it. What analysts of science call novelty, the
operators I shadowed called "the paperwork of surprise."

The distinction matters. When the Array's wideband receiver flagged an
unmodeled pulse in the third quarter of my fieldwork, nobody described the
pulse as novel. The overnight operator logged it as interference; the
calibration lead re-ran the pointing model; the visiting fellow drafted three
alternative mundane explanations before breakfast. Novelty entered the scene
only when the mundane explanations had been exhausted in front of the right
witnesses — and, crucially, the witnesses had to do visible work to fail to
explain the pulse away. I call this work audience repair: the collective
labor of stitching a torn expectation back together in a way that leaves a
seam. The seam is the novelty. A finding whose seam is invisible is not
experienced as novel at all; it is experienced as something everyone
supposedly already knew.

This reframing inverts the usual measurement strategy. If novelty were a
property of ideas, one could score it from texts alone, counting improbable
combinations of references or terms. If novelty is instead the residue of
audience repair, then scoring texts measures only the advertising of novelty,
not its accomplishment. At the Array, the most consequential novelty of the
decade — the recalibration episode I describe in Section 4 — produced a
publication whose abstract contained no first-of-its-kind language at all.
The operators had learned that claims of novelty invite audits, and audits
consume instrument time. Their solution was to let the user community
discover the seam for themselves, a strategy one interviewee summarized as
"never call it new, make them call it new."

Three implications organize the manuscript. First, novelty has a maintenance
schedule. Seams wear out; an achievement that no longer requires repair from
new audiences quietly stops being novel, which is why textbook renderings of
the recalibration episode now read as obvious. Second, novelty is rationed.
Because audience repair consumes attention that instrument communities budget
carefully, operators time their surprises to funding cycles — the Array's
management deferred announcing the pulse for one quarter so that the repair
work would land inside a review window. Third, novelty is insurable. Groups
hedge by distributing precursor mentions across low-stakes venues, so that a
failed surprise can be reframed as always-already anticipated, and a
successful one can be backdated.

None of this is cynicism about discovery. The pulse was real; the
recalibration mattered. The point is that what made them novel was not
located in them. Novelty, on the account developed here, is the visible
repair of a competent audience's expectations, performed with materials the
claiming group has strategically prepared. The Array's staff are not
manufacturing false surprises; they are manufacturing the conditions under
which a genuine surprise can be ratified without bankrupting anyone's
attention budget.

The draft proceeds as follows. Section 2 situates the argument against
accounts that locate novelty in recombination, in distance metrics, or in
citation disruption, and explains why each mistakes the advertisement for the
accomplishment. Section 3 describes the Array, my access, and the operations
logs. Section 4 reconstructs the recalibration episode as a sequence of
repair scenes. Section 5 develops the rationing and insurance mechanisms.
Section 6 considers scope conditions: audience repair should govern novelty
wherever expectations are institutionalized enough to tear visibly, which is
to say almost everywhere that science is funded, reviewed, and taught.
